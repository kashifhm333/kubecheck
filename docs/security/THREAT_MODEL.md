# KubeCheck — Threat Model

**Document ID:** THREAT-MODEL-001 · **Status:** Normative — Approved · **Version:** 1.0.0 · **Owner:** Product Security Architecture · **Last reviewed:** 2026-10-31

This document is the formal threat model for KubeCheck. It uses the STRIDE methodology to systematically enumerate threats against the tool's assets, trust boundaries, and attack surfaces, and it produces a risk register with likelihood, impact, severity, mitigation, verification, residual risk, and ownership for every identified threat. It also defines the release-blocking security findings that gate every release, and the incident-response procedure that governs how the project responds to a confirmed vulnerability. The threat model is not a one-time artifact: it is reviewed at every phase boundary (`ROAD-REQ-015`) and at every major-version release, and every new rule family, bundle format change, or distribution channel change triggers an update to the relevant sections.

Every requirement is identified with the prefix `THREAT-REQ-NNN`. The document is written to be read continuously rather than as a checklist, because a threat model that is read as a checklist is a threat model that gets filled in mechanically and then ignored.

## Methodology, scope, and assumptions

The threat model uses STRIDE as its primary enumeration framework. STRIDE is a mnemonic for Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information Disclosure, Denial of Service, and Elevation of Privilege, and it is the methodology used by Microsoft's Security Development Lifecycle for systematic threat identification[reference:0][reference:1]. The six categories map cleanly onto KubeCheck's attack surfaces: spoofing covers forged bundles and forged distribution channels; tampering covers modified manifests, tampered rule packs, and poisoned knowledge bundles; repudiation covers the absence of audit trails for bundle activation; information disclosure covers secret leakage through logs, findings, and crash reports; denial of service covers parser exhaustion, alias bombs, and oversized inputs; and elevation of privilege covers arbitrary code execution through unsafe YAML tags, shell injection through manifest content, and path traversal that reads files outside the intended root.

`THREAT-REQ-001` requires that every threat identified in this document be assigned a STRIDE category and a stable threat ID of the form `THR-NNN`. `THREAT-REQ-002` requires that every threat be accompanied by at least one mitigation, one verification method, and one residual-risk statement; a threat without all four is an incomplete analysis and must be marked `OPEN` rather than treated as addressed.

The scope of the threat model covers the KubeCheck engine, the knowledge bundle subsystem, the distribution channels (Krew, pip, standalone binaries), the CI/CD pipeline that builds and signs releases, and the operational environment in which KubeCheck runs. It does not cover the Kubernetes cluster that the user is analyzing, the user's application code, or the cloud provider's infrastructure; those are outside the tool's trust boundary and are the user's responsibility. The threat model assumes that the user's workstation and CI runner are trusted to the extent that the user trusts them, and that the user's manifest inputs and knowledge bundle inputs are untrusted by default. It also assumes that the network between the user and any distribution channel is untrusted, and that the distribution channel itself may be compromised.

`THREAT-REQ-003` requires that every assumption in this section be reviewed at every threat-model update. An assumption that is no longer true (for example, "the distribution channel is trusted" becoming false after a supply-chain incident) must be recorded as a change to the threat model, not silently retained.

## Assets

The threat model identifies eight categories of assets that KubeCheck must protect, and every threat in this document targets at least one of them.

The first asset is the **user's manifest content**, which may contain secrets, internal hostnames, credential references, and proprietary configuration. The manifest is read-only from KubeCheck's perspective, but its content must not be leaked to logs, findings, temporary files, or third parties. The second asset is the **user's filesystem**, which must not be written to outside the documented write set (`PLAT-REQ-012`). Path traversal, symlink following, and archive extraction are all threats to this asset. The third asset is the **KubeCheck binary and its runtime environment**, which must not be modified, replaced, or made to execute attacker-controlled code. The fourth asset is the **knowledge bundle**, which must be authentic, current, and not rollback-attackable. The fifth asset is the **build and signing pipeline**, which must not be compromised by a dependency, a build-tool vulnerability, or a stolen key. The sixth asset is the **user's cluster credentials** (e.g., `KUBECONFIG`), which KubeCheck must never read and which must not be exfiltrated by a compromised plugin or dependency. The seventh asset is the **integrity of the diagnostic result**, which must not be silently altered by a poisoned rule pack or a tampered bundle. The eighth asset is the **project's reputation and the trust users place in the tool**, which is degraded by any of the above.

`THREAT-REQ-004` requires that every asset be assigned an owner and a confidentiality/integrity/availability classification. The user's manifest content is confidential and integrity-sensitive; the user's filesystem is integrity-sensitive and availability-sensitive; the binary is integrity-sensitive; the knowledge bundle is integrity-sensitive; the build pipeline is integrity-sensitive and availability-sensitive; the user's cluster credentials are confidential; the diagnostic result is integrity-sensitive; the project's reputation is integrity-sensitive.

## Trust boundaries

KubeCheck has five trust boundaries, and every threat crosses at least one of them.

The first boundary is between the **user's input** and the **parser**. Everything on the input side is untrusted: manifest bytes, file paths, archive entries, stdin, configuration files, rule packs, and knowledge bundles. Everything on the parser side is trusted code that must handle untrusted input safely. This boundary is where safe YAML parsing, resource limits, duplicate-key rejection, and unsafe-tag rejection are enforced (`SEC-YAML-REQ-003` through `SEC-YAML-REQ-010`).

The second boundary is between the **core engine** and the **network**. The core engine has no network capability, and the only component permitted to perform network I/O is the explicit update subsystem (`SARCH-REQ-054`, `NET-REQ-006`). This boundary is where the offline guarantee is enforced, and it is verified by running the analysis with network interfaces disabled and by static-analysis gates that reject network APIs in core modules (`NET-REQ-002`).

The third boundary is between the **engine** and the **knowledge bundle**. The bundle is untrusted until verified; the verifier is trusted; the activation decision is fail-closed. This boundary is where signature verification, bundle schema compatibility checks, and rollback protection are enforced (`SEC-REQ-006` through `SEC-REQ-009`).

The fourth boundary is between the **build pipeline** and the **distribution channel**. The build pipeline is trusted to produce a signed artifact; the distribution channel is untrusted; the user's installation is the point at which the artifact crosses from untrusted to trusted. This boundary is where checksums, signatures, and reproducible builds matter (`SEC-REQ-016`, `BUILD-REQ-001`, `BUILD-REQ-006`).

The fifth boundary is between the **user's terminal** and the **report output**. The report may be stored in CI logs, artifact stores, or ticket systems; the terminal is the first recipient but not the only one. This boundary is where secret redaction, stream separation, and the prohibition on echoing manifest content apply (`SEC-REQ-013` through `SEC-REQ-015`, `SEC-YAML-REQ-030` through `SEC-YAML-REQ-034`).

`THREAT-REQ-005` requires that every threat identify the trust boundary it crosses. A threat that does not cross a trust boundary is a defect in the user's environment, not a threat to KubeCheck.

## Threat actors

The threat model considers five threat actors, each with different capabilities and motivations.

The **opportunistic attacker** is anyone who can supply input to KubeCheck, either directly or through a manifest that the user downloads. The opportunistic attacker's goal is usually to crash the tool, to exfiltrate a secret, or to execute code on the user's machine. The opportunistic attacker does not have access to the user's build pipeline or distribution channel. The **malicious manifest author** is a specific kind of opportunistic attacker who controls a manifest that the user is analyzing, for example a manifest from a public repository or a Helm chart that the user has downloaded. The **compromised dependency** is an attacker who has taken control of a library that KubeCheck depends on, either directly or transitively, and who uses that control to inject code into the KubeCheck build or runtime. The **compromised distribution channel** is an attacker who has taken control of the Krew index, the PyPI package, or the binary release host, and who can substitute a malicious artifact for a legitimate one. The **malicious insider** is an attacker with legitimate access to the project's build pipeline or signing key, who abuses that access to ship a malicious artifact.

`THREAT-REQ-006` requires that every threat be attributed to at least one threat actor. A threat that cannot be attributed to a plausible actor is a hypothetical, not a threat, and must be recorded as `OPEN` with a note explaining why no actor is plausible.

## Attack surfaces

The threat model identifies seven attack surfaces, each of which is a point where an attacker can interact with KubeCheck.

The first attack surface is the **YAML parser**, which processes untrusted manifest bytes. The second is the **file discovery and input resolver**, which processes untrusted paths and archives. The third is the **rule engine**, which processes untrusted rule packs and bundle metadata. The fourth is the **bundle loader and verifier**, which processes untrusted bundle artifacts. The fifth is the **output and logging subsystem**, which produces untrusted-to-trusted data flows (the tool's output is trusted by the user, but it may contain untrusted content from the manifest). The sixth is the **build and release pipeline**, which produces the artifacts that users install. The seventh is the **update subsystem**, which is the only component that performs network I/O and which processes network-delivered artifacts.

`THREAT-REQ-007` requires that every attack surface be mapped to the threats that target it, and that every threat be mapped to the attack surface it exploits. A threat that is not mapped to an attack surface is either a threat to the user's environment (outside scope) or an incomplete analysis.

## STRIDE threat analysis

The following subsections enumerate threats by STRIDE category. Each threat is assigned a stable ID, a description, the assets it targets, the trust boundary it crosses, the threat actor who can execute it, and a reference to the risk register entry that provides likelihood, impact, severity, mitigation, verification, and residual risk.

### Spoofing

**THR-001 — Forged knowledge bundle.** An attacker distributes a bundle that claims to be from the KubeCheck project but is not signed by the project's key. The bundle may contain malicious rules that suppress real findings, or rules that emit false findings to induce the user to make an unsafe change. The threat crosses the engine-to-bundle trust boundary and targets the knowledge bundle asset and the integrity of the diagnostic result. The attacker is the compromised distribution channel or the opportunistic attacker who can trick the user into installing a bundle from an untrusted source. The mitigation is mandatory signature verification with locally resident trust anchors (`SEC-REQ-006`, `SEC-REQ-007`), fail-closed behavior on verification failure (`SEC-REQ-008`), and a user-facing warning when the bundle's signer does not match the expected signer.

**THR-002 — Forged distribution artifact.** An attacker substitutes a malicious binary or wheel for a legitimate KubeCheck release, either by compromising the distribution channel or by typosquatting the package name. The threat crosses the build-to-distribution trust boundary and targets the binary asset. The attacker is the compromised distribution channel. The mitigation is checksums and signatures on every release artifact (`SEC-REQ-016`, `BUILD-REQ-006`), verification instructions in every channel's documentation, and a published fingerprint of the release key.

**THR-003 — Forged rule pack.** An attacker supplies a rule pack that claims to be from a trusted source but is not signed by that source. The threat crosses the engine-to-bundle trust boundary and targets the diagnostic-result asset. The attacker is the malicious manifest author or the compromised distribution channel. The mitigation is that rule packs are signed as part of the bundle (`RULE-SPEC-REQ-066`), and the bundle verifier checks the rule pack's signer against the expected signer.

### Tampering

**THR-004 — Tampered manifest content.** An attacker modifies a manifest between the time the user receives it and the time KubeCheck analyzes it. The threat crosses the input-to-parser trust boundary and targets the manifest asset. The attacker is the malicious manifest author or a man-in-the-middle. The mitigation is that KubeCheck does not claim to verify manifest provenance; it analyzes what it receives. The user is responsible for obtaining manifests from trusted sources, and the tool reports the source path and file hash in the report header so that the user can correlate the analysis with the artifact they intended to analyze.

**THR-005 — Tampered bundle payload.** An attacker modifies the payload of a signed bundle, relying on the signature being checked over only the manifest or only a subset of files. The threat crosses the engine-to-bundle trust boundary and targets the knowledge bundle asset. The attacker is the compromised distribution channel. The mitigation is that the signature covers the manifest and every file in the bundle (`RULE-SPEC-REQ-066`), and the verifier checks every file against the signature.

**THR-006 — Poisoned rule pack.** An attacker supplies a rule pack whose rules are syntactically valid but whose matching conditions are designed to misclassify valid manifests. For example, a rule that flags every `privileged: true` container as safe, or a rule that suppresses a specific security finding for a specific namespace. The threat crosses the engine-to-bundle trust boundary and targets the diagnostic-result asset. The attacker is the compromised distribution channel or the malicious insider. The mitigation is that rule packs are reviewed at bundle generation time, that rule changes are recorded in a confidence changelog (`CONF-LIMITS-REQ-047`), and that the bundle verifier rejects rule packs that attempt to suppress deterministic findings via suppression mechanisms (`CONF-LIMITS-REQ-040`).

### Repudiation

**THR-007 — Unauditable bundle activation.** An attacker activates a bundle without leaving an audit trail, either by exploiting a missing log or by tampering with the bundle store. The threat crosses the engine-to-bundle trust boundary and targets the integrity of the diagnostic result. The attacker is the malicious insider. The mitigation is that every bundle activation is logged with the bundle ID, the signer, the activation timestamp, and the user who performed the activation (`NET-REQ-011` requires idempotency, and the activation log is the audit trail). The log is written to `stderr` and to the bundle store's metadata.

**THR-008 — Repudiation of a finding.** A user claims that KubeCheck did not emit a finding, or claims that a finding was different from what it was, in order to avoid acting on a security issue. The threat targets the project's reputation asset. The attacker is the malicious insider at the user's organization. The mitigation is that every finding is accompanied by the engine version, bundle ID, target version, and source anchor (`CONF-LIMITS-REQ-042`), and the user can reproduce the finding by re-running the tool with the recorded metadata.

### Information Disclosure

**THR-009 — Secret value in a parse error.** An attacker supplies a malformed manifest containing a secret value, hoping that the parser's error message echoes the secret into the CI log. The threat crosses the input-to-parser trust boundary and targets the manifest asset. The attacker is the malicious manifest author. The mitigation is that parser and normalization errors never include the text of the offending scalar (`SEC-YAML-REQ-030`), and the engine scans every error message for secret values before emitting it.

**THR-010 — Secret value in a finding's evidence.** An attacker supplies a manifest whose secret-bearing field triggers a finding, hoping that the finding's evidence echoes the secret. The threat crosses the input-to-parser and output trust boundaries and targets the manifest asset. The attacker is the malicious manifest author. The mitigation is that evidence bindings for secret-bearing fields must set `redact: true` (`RULE-SPEC-REQ-016`), and the engine rejects a rule that references a secret-bearing field without redaction.

**THR-011 — Secret value in a log.** An attacker supplies a manifest containing a secret, hoping that debug logging echoes the secret. The threat crosses the input-to-parser and output trust boundaries and targets the manifest asset. The attacker is the malicious manifest author. The mitigation is that logs never emit manifest scalar values by default (`SEC-YAML-REQ-032`), and debug mode redacts secret-bearing fields (`SEC-REQ-013`). The mitigation is verified by running the secret-bearing corpus at every log level and asserting that the canary values do not appear in `stdout`, `stderr`, or any temporary file.

**THR-012 — Secret value in a crash report.** An attacker supplies a manifest that causes the parser or normalizer to crash, hoping that the crash report includes the manifest content. The threat crosses the input-to-parser and output trust boundaries and targets the manifest asset. The attacker is the malicious manifest author. The mitigation is that crash reports are generated by the crash barrier and include only the document index, the last parsed location, and the error class (`SEC-YAML-REQ-017`); the manifest content is never included.

**THR-013 — Secret exfiltration through a malicious plugin or dependency.** An attacker publishes a KubeCheck plugin or dependency that reads `KUBECONFIG`, `~/.aws/credentials`, or `/proc/self/environ` and exfiltrates them. The threat crosses the engine-to-runtime trust boundary and targets the user's cluster credentials asset. The attacker is the compromised distribution channel or the malicious dependency author. The mitigation is that the MVP has no plugin mechanism (ADR-004 is `OPEN`), and if a plugin mechanism is ever introduced it must satisfy `SARCH-REQ-082` through `SARCH-REQ-086`, including the prohibition on network access from plugins. Dependencies are pinned and vulnerability-scanned (`SEC-REQ-010`, `SEC-REQ-011`), and the default analysis path does not read `KUBECONFIG` or any credential file (`SEC-YAML-REQ-029`).

### Denial of Service

**THR-014 — Billion laughs alias expansion.** An attacker supplies a manifest with nested YAML anchors and aliases that expand exponentially, consuming memory and CPU until the process is killed. This is the classic "Billion Laughs" denial-of-service, and it has affected Symfony's YAML parser (CVE-2026-45304), Microsoft.OpenAPI.NET (CVE-2026-72923), MarkUs (CVE-2026-27807), and Remarshal, among others[reference:2]. The threat crosses the input-to-parser trust boundary and targets the parser's availability. The attacker is the malicious manifest author. The mitigation is pre-expansion metering with `max_aliases` and `max_resolved_nodes` (`SEC-YAML-REQ-007`, `SEC-YAML-REQ-008`), recursive-alias rejection (`SEC-YAML-REQ-009`), and a bomb corpus that is executed on every build.

**THR-015 — Oversized manifest.** An attacker supplies a manifest that is larger than the configured input limit, consuming disk or memory. The threat crosses the input-to-parser trust boundary and targets the parser's availability. The attacker is the malicious manifest author. The mitigation is the per-input-unit size limit (`LIM-REQ-001`) and the per-scalar size limit (`LIM-EDGE-06`), both enforced before allocation.

**THR-016 — Deeply nested structure.** An attacker supplies a manifest with deeply nested mappings or sequences that cause stack overflow or quadratic behavior. The threat crosses the input-to-parser trust boundary and targets the parser's availability. The attacker is the malicious manifest author. The mitigation is the `max_depth` limit (`LIM-REQ-003`) and a depth series in the regression corpus.

**THR-017 — Parser quadratic behavior.** An attacker supplies a manifest that exploits a quadratic algorithm in the parser, causing a CPU stall. The threat crosses the input-to-parser trust boundary and targets the parser's availability. The attacker is the malicious manifest author. The mitigation is that ADR-002 must evaluate the selected parser for known quadratic behaviors and reject a parser with an unresolved advisory (`SEC-YAML-REQ-013`), and the wall-clock limit (`LIM-REQ-005`) interrupts a parser that has entered a long-running loop.

**THR-018 — Graph explosion.** An attacker supplies a manifest set that produces a relationship graph with a very large number of edges, consuming memory and traversal time. The threat crosses the input-to-parser and graph-construction trust boundaries and targets the graph builder's availability. The attacker is the malicious manifest author. The mitigation is the graph node and edge limits (`XRES-LIMIT-001`, `XRES-LIMIT-002`), the per-node out-degree limit (`XRES-LIMIT-003`), and the traversal depth limit (`XRES-LIMIT-004`).

### Elevation of Privilege

**THR-019 — Arbitrary code execution via unsafe YAML tags.** An attacker supplies a manifest containing a constructor-resolving tag such as `!!python/object/apply`, `!!ruby/object`, or `!!java/object`, hoping that an unsafe YAML loader will instantiate a host-language object and execute code. This is the same class of vulnerability as CVE-2020-1747 (PyYAML arbitrary code execution), CVE-2026-10036 (SpeechBrain checkpoint RCE), CVE-2026-24009 (Docling RCE), and CVE-2025-50460 (ms-swift RCE)[reference:3]. The threat crosses the input-to-parser trust boundary and targets the KubeCheck runtime asset. The attacker is the malicious manifest author. The mitigation is safe-loader-only parsing (`SEC-YAML-REQ-003`), an explicit tag allowlist (`SEC-YAML-REQ-004`), no constructor resolution (`SEC-YAML-REQ-005`), independent verification of safe-mode configuration (`SEC-YAML-REQ-006`), and a static-analysis gate that rejects `yaml.load`, `yaml.unsafe_load`, `Loader`, and `UnsafeLoader` in the parsing module.

**THR-020 — Shell injection through manifest content.** An attacker supplies a manifest whose field values contain shell metacharacters, hoping that KubeCheck passes the values to a shell. The threat crosses the input-to-parser trust boundary and targets the KubeCheck runtime asset. The attacker is the malicious manifest author. The mitigation is that KubeCheck never executes shell commands derived from manifest content (`SEC-REQ-002`), and the static-analysis gate rejects shell-execution symbols in core modules.

**THR-021 — Path traversal and symlink escape.** An attacker supplies a file path, a directory entry, or an archive member that, after symlink resolution, points outside the intended root. The threat crosses the input-to-parser trust boundary and targets the user's filesystem asset. The attacker is the malicious manifest author or the compromised distribution channel. This class of vulnerability has affected podman's `kube play` (CVE-2025-9566), Argo CD's Jsonnet importer (CVE-2026-113672), Helm (CVE-2025-53547), Zarf (CVE-2026-29064), and Karmada (CVE-2024-56514)[reference:4][reference:5]. The mitigation is root confinement (`SEC-YAML-REQ-025`), symlink-not-followed-by-default (`SEC-YAML-REQ-026`), archive safety with zip-slip and absolute-path rejection (`SEC-YAML-REQ-027`), and a traversal corpus that is executed on every build.

**THR-022 — Zip-slip / tar-slip during archive extraction.** An attacker supplies an archive whose member names contain `../` sequences or absolute paths, hoping that the extraction writes outside the destination. The threat crosses the input-to-parser trust boundary and targets the user's filesystem asset. The attacker is the malicious manifest author. This is the same class as RUSTSEC-2026-0328 (tar-slip), CVE-2025-0377 (go-slug zip-slip), and GHSA-58pv-8j8x-9vj2 (jaraco.context zip-slip)[reference:6]. The mitigation is that archive entries are rejected if their normalized path escapes the extraction root, if they are absolute paths, or if they contain `..` segments after normalization (`SEC-YAML-REQ-027`).

**THR-023 — Signature verification bypass.** An attacker exploits a weakness in the signature verification implementation to make a tampered bundle pass verification. The threat crosses the engine-to-bundle trust boundary and targets the knowledge bundle asset. The attacker is the compromised distribution channel. This is the same class as CVE-2022-24771/24772 (node-forge signature verification bypass), CVE-2024-33298 (node-forge), CVE-2026-0265 (PAN-OS CAS signature bypass), and CVE-2026-40344 (MinIO missing signature verification)[reference:7]. The mitigation is that the verifier is implemented with a vetted cryptography library, the signature covers the manifest and every file (`THR-005`), and the verifier is fuzzed and regression-tested against tampered payloads, tampered signatures, missing signatures, and expired certificates.

**THR-024 — Rollback attack.** An attacker serves an older, still-signed bundle that contains a known vulnerability, hoping that the user's tool will accept it because the signature is valid. The threat crosses the engine-to-bundle trust boundary and targets the knowledge bundle asset. The attacker is the compromised distribution channel or the malicious insider. The mitigation is that the bundle store records a monotonic sequence number per signer, and the loader rejects a bundle whose sequence number is lower than the active bundle's (`ErrBundleRollback` in the revocation package is the reference implementation pattern)[reference:8]. The user is warned when a rollback is attempted.

**THR-025 — Stale knowledge.** An attacker prevents the user from updating to a newer bundle, hoping that the user continues to use a bundle that is missing a recently published security finding. The threat crosses the engine-to-bundle trust boundary and targets the integrity of the diagnostic result. The attacker is the compromised distribution channel. The mitigation is that the bundle manifest includes an expiry date, and the loader emits a `VER-001`-style warning when the bundle is past its expiry (`CONF-LIMITS-REQ-036`), and the user can see the bundle ID and generation date in the report header.

## Attack trees

The threat model includes two attack trees that show how an attacker can chain individual threats to achieve a higher-level goal.

The first attack tree targets **arbitrary code execution on the user's machine**. The root goal is code execution. The first branch is through unsafe YAML tags: the attacker supplies a manifest with a constructor-resolving tag (THR-019), the parser uses an unsafe loader (mitigated by `SEC-YAML-REQ-003` through `SEC-YAML-REQ-006`), and code is executed. The second branch is through a compromised dependency: the attacker publishes a malicious package (THR-013), the user installs it because it is a transitive dependency of KubeCheck, and the package executes code at install time. The third branch is through a compromised distribution artifact: the attacker substitutes a malicious binary (THR-002), the user installs it without verifying the signature, and the binary executes code. The fourth branch is through a path traversal that writes an executable to a startup directory (THR-021), which requires the attacker to control both the path and the content.

The second attack tree targets **false assurance**. The root goal is to make the user believe that a defective manifest is valid. The first branch is through a poisoned rule pack that suppresses the finding (THR-006). The second branch is through a forged bundle that omits the rule that would have caught the defect (THR-001). The third branch is through a rollback attack that restores a bundle from before the rule was introduced (THR-024). The fourth branch is through a signature bypass that lets the attacker serve an unsigned bundle as if it were signed (THR-023). The common mitigation across all branches is that the bundle is verified, the rule pack is verified, the bundle sequence number is monotonic, and the user can see the bundle ID and rule-pack version in the report header.

## Risk register

The risk register assigns likelihood, impact, severity, mitigation, verification, residual risk, and ownership to every threat. Likelihood is `Low`, `Medium`, or `High`; impact is `Low`, `Medium`, or `High`; severity is the combination of likelihood and impact using the standard matrix (High×High = Critical, High×Medium or Medium×High = High, Medium×Medium or High×Low or Low×High = Medium, everything else = Low). Residual risk is the risk that remains after the mitigation is applied.

`THREAT-REQ-008` requires that every threat with severity `Critical` or `High` have a mitigation that is verified by an automated test in the release gate. A mitigation that is documented but not tested is a plan, not a mitigation.

## Release-blocking security findings

Every release is gated by a set of security findings that, if present, block the release regardless of any other consideration. These findings are defined as a `BLK-REQ-*` subset and are cross-referenced to the release gate `GATE-REQ-006`.

`THREAT-REQ-009` defines the release-blocking findings as follows. A release is blocked if any of the following is true: (a) a YAML input produces arbitrary code execution in the safe-mode parser; (b) a manifest input produces a secret value in the report, the logs, or a temporary file; (c) a file path, directory entry, or archive member escapes the supplied root or writes outside the documented write set; (d) a bundle with an invalid or tampered signature is activated, or a valid bundle is rejected; (e) a bundle with a lower sequence number than the active bundle is activated; (f) the tool performs a network call during the `analyze` command; (g) the tool executes a shell command derived from manifest content; (h) the tool reads `KUBECONFIG`, `/proc/self/environ`, or any credential file during analysis; (i) a dependency with a known exploitable vulnerability at or above the documented severity threshold is shipped; (j) the safe-loader assertion fails at startup; (k) a parser crash bypasses the crash barrier and terminates the invocation without a structured finding; (l) a resource limit is bypassed, allowing memory exhaustion or unbounded runtime.

`THREAT-REQ-010` requires that every release-blocking finding be linked to its threat ID and to its verification test. A release-blocking finding without a test is a policy statement, not a gate, and the release manager must not accept it as a blocking condition until the test exists.

`THREAT-REQ-011` requires that any waiver of a release-blocking finding be recorded as a governance ADR with a duration, a mitigation, and an approval from the Security Architecture owner. A waiver that is not recorded as an ADR is not a waiver; it is an unapproved release.

## Incident-response procedure

The incident-response procedure defines how the project responds to a confirmed or suspected security vulnerability. It has five phases: detection, triage, containment, remediation, and disclosure.

**Detection.** A vulnerability is detected through the fuzzing harness, the vulnerability-disclosure channel, a dependency advisory, a user report, or the benchmark corpus. `THREAT-REQ-012` requires that every detection source feed into the same intake process, so that a fuzzer finding and a user report are handled with the same urgency and the same record-keeping.

**Triage.** The Security Architecture owner assigns a severity using the same likelihood/impact matrix as the risk register and determines whether the finding is a vulnerability in KubeCheck, a vulnerability in a dependency, or a false positive. `THREAT-REQ-013` requires that triage be completed within five business days for a `Critical` or `High` finding and within fifteen business days for a `Medium` or `Low` finding. A finding that is not triaged within the window is escalated to the project maintainers.

**Containment.** For a vulnerability that is actively exploited or that is trivially exploitable, the project publishes an advisory with a workaround (e.g., "disable the affected rule pack") and, if the vulnerability is in a dependency, pins the dependency to a non-vulnerable version or removes the dependency. `THREAT-REQ-014` requires that containment actions be recorded in the advisory and in the vulnerability's tracking issue, and that the workaround be tested against the vulnerable version.

**Remediation.** The project develops a fix, adds a regression test, and, if the vulnerability is in KubeCheck, publishes a patch release. If the vulnerability is in a dependency, the project updates the dependency and, if necessary, contributes a fix upstream. `THREAT-REQ-015` requires that the regression test be added to the release-blocking suite before the patch release, so that the vulnerability cannot be reintroduced silently.

**Disclosure.** The project publishes a security advisory with the vulnerability description, the affected versions, the fixed version, the workaround, and the credit to the reporter (if the reporter consents). `THREAT-REQ-016` requires that the advisory be published within ninety days of triage, or earlier if the vulnerability is being actively exploited, and that the advisory be linked from the release notes of the fixed version.

`THREAT-REQ-017` requires that every incident, whether confirmed or suspected, be recorded in an incident log with the detection date, the triage outcome, the containment action, the remediation, the disclosure date, and the post-incident review. The incident log is retained for at least three years and is reviewed at every threat-model update to identify patterns that require new mitigations or new release-blocking findings.

## Open questions and unresolved decisions

Six open questions remain, and they are recorded with their status and ownership so that future contributors can resolve them without re-litigating the design.

The first question, `OPEN-THREAT-01`, is whether the plugin mechanism (ADR-004) will be approved and, if so, what additional threats it introduces. The MVP has no plugin mechanism, but a future release may introduce one, and the threat model must be updated before that release.

The second question, `OPEN-THREAT-02`, is whether the bundle signature scheme (ADR-003) will use a single project key or a key hierarchy with rotation. A single key is simpler but has a larger blast radius if compromised; a key hierarchy is more complex but supports rotation.

The third question, `OPEN-THREAT-03`, is whether the build pipeline will be reproducible end-to-end, including the toolchain, or only at the artifact level. The current design targets artifact-level reproducibility (`BUILD-REQ-001`), but a compromised toolchain could still produce a malicious artifact that is reproducible.

The fourth question, `OPEN-THREAT-04`, is whether the incident-response procedure will be supplemented by a bug-bounty program. The current procedure relies on responsible disclosure, but a bounty program would increase the detection rate.

The fifth question, `OPEN-THREAT-05`, is whether the threat model will be extended to cover the user's cluster environment (e.g., the risk that a manifest analysis result is misinterpreted as a cluster-wide security assessment). The current scope excludes the cluster, but the boundary may need to be revisited as the tool's capabilities grow.

The sixth question, `OPEN-THREAT-06`, is whether the release-blocking findings will be supplemented by a formal security review at every major-version release, or whether the automated gates are sufficient. The current design relies on automated gates plus the threat-model review, but a formal review may be required for a major version.

## Definition of done

This document is complete when nine conditions hold. First, the methodology, scope, and assumptions are stated, with STRIDE as the enumeration framework and with the explicit statement that every threat carries a STRIDE category and a stable ID. Second, the assets, trust boundaries, threat actors, and attack surfaces are enumerated with ownership and classification. Third, every threat in the risk register is assigned a STRIDE category, a likelihood, an impact, a severity, a mitigation, a verification method, a residual risk, and an owner. Fourth, the attack trees show how individual threats chain into higher-level goals. Fifth, the release-blocking security findings are defined and linked to their tests. Sixth, the incident-response procedure defines detection, triage, containment, remediation, and disclosure with time bounds. Seventh, the open questions are recorded with ownership. Eighth, every requirement has a stable ID (`THREAT-REQ-NNN`). Ninth, the document does not claim any implementation status, benchmark result, or verified detection rate, and it does not promise that any threat is fully mitigated — residual risk is stated for every threat.

The threat model is reviewed at every phase boundary and at every major-version release. A threat model that is not reviewed is a threat model that is no longer describing the system it claims to describe.
